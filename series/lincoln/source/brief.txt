What we're making
A true-history drama series for the Today In History app: about 30 episodes of about a minute each. These are short films in a series, not one-off clips and not ads. People binge them, so every episode has to make them tap the next one.

Faces and dialogue
	•	67% of lines are said on the speaker's face, lip-synced in close-up. 27% play over a reaction or off-screen; 6% are inner voice.
	•	Someone is talking about 55% of the time.
	•	Lines are short: about 5 words, almost never over 12.
	•	Close-ups dominate. Wide shots set a room once, then it's faces, eyes, hands and reactions.
Structure
	•	Episode 1 opens cold, often on a flash-forward plus a time card ("72 hours earlier").
	•	Later episodes open on the previous episode's cliff (72% of them).
	•	One or two scenes per episode. One room, two faces in conflict, a secret.
	•	Cliffhangers, from most to least common: a reveal, a decision, a threat, an arrival, violence, a kiss or touch, a deal, a humiliation.
On screen, in every series
	•	Burned-in captions.
	•	A name card with a role on each character's first appearance.
	•	Inserts of a phone, letter or object that matters.
Common touches
	•	Slow motion at the peak moments.
	•	Music stings and sound hits on every reveal.
	•	A white flash on the last frame.
Our difference: it all really happened.
The few hard rules
	1	It's true. No invented facts. Dramatized dialogue is fine. Lines marked REAL WORDS are said exactly as written.
	2	Each episode runs 60–90 s. Vertical 9:16 (1080×1920).
	3	Same face, same voice. Each character looks and sounds like the same person in every shot and every episode.
	4	Story from the first second. No long title cards or slow intros.
	5	Every episode ends on a hook that makes you want the next one.
	6	Burned-in captions, readable on a phone.

Lincoln: The Conspiracy: Season 1, episodes 1–5
Status: v1, 2026-11-22. Writing only, $0. This follows the format update of 2026-11-21 (FLAGSHIPS.md, last section): about 30 episodes of about 60 s each. Layout follows GARBO-SEASON-30x60.md §3. Clip tables follow OURS-MAX-HEADROOM-EP.md. Facts, cast and look come from series/lincoln-the-conspiracy.md. Nothing here is locked until the checks in §5 are done.
For the pipeline that films this:
	•	One clip, one setup. Each clip is one situation in one place, with no time jump inside it. Stay inside the clip's place and framing.
	•	Text is never on a prop. Every word the viewer reads (name cards, TRUE cards, time supers, tags, captions) is a graphic overlay. Letters, cards and newspapers are shown soft or edge-on, and their words come through a voice.
	•	No title card and no end card. A 1 s corner bug ("LINCOLN: THE CONSPIRACY · 1") sits over live picture at 0:00–0:01. Each episode ends on its freeze plus TRUE card. Nothing comes after it.
	•	No flash-forward cold opens. This follows the Garbo format. The v2 cold open (the laugh line in the box) previewed the payoff, so it is dropped. Each hook comes from inside the episode's first scene.
	•	Delivery legend:
	•	face: on screen and lip-synced.
	•	off: spoken over a reaction shot or from off screen.
	•	inner: the character's thoughts, set in italic captions.
	•	Tag legend:
	•	REAL WORDS · year: documented wording, quoted exactly.
	•	REPORTED · …: a recollection or a later report of what someone said. The tag shows the chain when it is second- or third-hand.
	•	TRUE card: a documented fact, stamped full width.
	•	Untagged lines are written for the drama, and no untagged line ever closes an episode.

1. Season premise and the 30-episode arc
Premise: On April 11, 1865, two days after Lee's surrender, a famous young actor stands in the crowd outside the White House and hears Lincoln support the vote for some Black men. Three days later he has a plan: the President, the Vice President and the Secretary of State, on one night, at one hour. General Grant would have made it four, but he left town that afternoon. Everyone knows how the night ends. Almost nobody knows the road to it:
	•	a boarding-house widow carrying a package to a country tavern;
	•	a calling card left at the Vice President's hotel;
	•	a hit man who went to the bar instead;
	•	a knife in a sick man's bedroom;
	•	after the night, a 12-day manhunt and a conspirator who got away for two years.
Where eps 1–5 sit: they are the first half of the countdown, from April 11 to 8 pm on April 14. Together they set up:
	•	the motive;
	•	the box, and Grant's refusal;
	•	the weapons;
	•	the card;
	•	the plan itself: three targets at one hour.
They spend none of the payoff. The shot comes in ep 12, after the late wall. The knife, Lincoln's death and the manhunt come after that.
Paywall:
	•	The early wall is after ep 5 (founder's candidate). Ep 5 has the strongest cliff of the first five. The whole plan is revealed: the President, the Vice President, and the Secretary of State in his sickbed, at about 10:15. The TRUE card names all three. The viewer knows the night is coming, and every road to it is behind the wall.
	•	The late wall is after ep 11. Ep 11 closes on real words, the play's laugh line, with the hammer drawn back. If the wall moves to ep 5, ep 11 stays the season's biggest held breath.

4. What's real, compressed and dramatized (per episode)
Ep 1: The Last Speech
Documented:
	•	Lincoln's last public address, on April 11, 1865, from the window above the north portico, at night, in drizzle, to a crowd celebrating Lee's surrender.
	•	Both of Lincoln's lines are quoted exactly from the address (Collected Works, ed. Basler). The super "HE MEANS THE VOTE. FOR SOME BLACK MEN." is our gloss of "the elective franchise … given to the colored man".
	•	A companion held the candle and Tad gathered the dropped pages (Noah Brooks's account).
	•	Booth was in the crowd.
Reported:
	•	Booth urging Powell to shoot Lincoln there, Powell refusing, "That means … citizenship" and "That is the last speech he will ever make" are all third-hand: Thomas Eckert, testifying in 1867 to what Powell told him while a prisoner. The tag on screen says THIRD-HAND.
	•	"That means … citizenship" is shortened. The reported line has a racial slur before "citizenship". We cut it, mark the cut with an ellipsis on screen, and disclose it here. The meaning is unchanged.
Dramatized:
	•	Every Booth–Powell exchange in clips 1 and 3. Their substance (Booth wants the shot, Powell refuses) follows Eckert, and the wording is ours.
	•	Herold, who some accounts place there too, is left out to keep the cast small.
The cliff card:
	•	"Three days later, he picked three targets" compresses when the plan formed. The three-target plan for April 14 is documented (trial, Atzerodt).
	•	When Booth turned kidnap into murder that week is debated. The card claims the plan, not the moment he decided.
Ep 2: The Box
Documented:
	•	On the morning of April 14, the White House reserved the state box at Ford's for the President's party.
	•	Grant attended the cabinet meeting that morning and declined the theatre, leaving with Julia that evening to see their children in Burlington, New Jersey.
	•	Booth had his mail sent to Ford's and learned around noon that the President was coming.
	•	Lincoln saw Booth in The Marble Heart at Ford's on November 9, 1863.
Accounts say:
	•	That it was Harry Ford who told Booth.
	•	That the invitation to Grant was made in person at the meeting.
Dramatized:
	•	All dialogue. Grant's reason is his documented one, put in our words. Lincoln's "The people want to see you" is ours.
	•	That Mary Lincoln had snubbed Julia Grant weeks earlier, at City Point, is often given as the real reason. It is left out of the episode because the accounts are secondhand.
	•	The empty auditorium and Booth looking up at the box are staged. The flags on the box are not shown, because they were hung later that afternoon.
Ep 3: Shooting Irons
Documented (trial record, Pitman 1865):
	•	Booth called at the Surratt boarding house in the afternoon and left a parcel with Mary Surratt. Lloyd testified that it held Booth's field glasses.
	•	Louis Weichmann drove her to Surrattsville in a hired buggy. Her stated business was a debt owed to her (John Nothey).
	•	Lloyd arrived at the tavern around 5 pm, having been drinking.
	•	Two carbines had been hidden at the tavern weeks earlier.
Reported:
	•	Her words to Lloyd come only from Lloyd's testimony, which gives them in indirect speech. We render them as direct speech and tag them REPORTED.
	•	Lloyd is a contested witness. He was drinking that day, and he testified under threat of prosecution. Defenders of Mary Surratt dispute his account. Her guilt is argued to this day, and the series says so in ep 25.
Dramatized:
	•	Every untagged line, including "Something breakable", "Drive, Mr. Weichmann" and "For who?".
	•	Booth's instruction "Only Lloyd": the parcel's delivery to Lloyd is documented, and Booth's wording is ours.
Ep 4: The Card
Documented:
	•	The card's text: "Don't wish to disturb you. Are you at home? J. Wilkes Booth." It survives at the National Archives.
	•	Booth left it at the Kirkwood House desk on the afternoon of April 14, and it was placed in the box of Johnson's private secretary, William A. Browning.
	•	Browning testified that he found it on returning and that he knew Booth.
	•	Atzerodt registered at the Kirkwood House that morning.
Open, and kept open:
	•	Whether Booth meant the card for Johnson or for Browning, and why he left it.
	•	The episode asks the question and doesn't answer it.
Dramatized:
	•	The clerk's lines and Booth's questions at the desk, though the clerk's testimony supports that Booth asked who was in.
	•	Booth murmuring the card's words as he writes. The words are the card's, and the murmuring is ours.
	•	"I've met him. In Nashville." paraphrases Browning's testimony.
	•	Atzerodt's scene: his room is documented, and the footsteps and the drink are staged. Nothing implies he knew the plan yet. By his own account, he learned of the killing at the 8 pm meeting (ep 5).
Ep 5: Ten Fifteen
Documented:
	•	On the afternoon of April 14, Booth gave his fellow actor John Matthews a sealed letter for the National Intelligencer. Matthews later said he read it and burned it after the assassination.
	•	Grant and Julia left Washington by the evening train.
	•	The 8 pm meeting at the Herndon House and the three-target plan: Booth would take Lincoln, Powell Seward and Atzerodt Johnson, at about 10:15. This rests on Atzerodt's statements and trial testimony.
	•	Seward was bedridden after his carriage accident of April 5.
	•	Booth carried a single-shot Derringer.
Reported:
	•	The rider at the carriage is Julia Grant's later recollection (her memoirs). She did not identify him at the time, and the link to Booth is hers, made afterwards. The super quotes no words, only that she wrote of it.
	•	That Grant's carriage passed while Booth was with Matthews comes from Matthews's later account.
	•	"I won't kill anybody" is the substance of Atzerodt's own statements, in which he says he refused. It is self-serving, from a man trying to save his neck, and it is tagged REPORTED.
Dramatized:
	•	All untagged lines.
	•	Booth pinning Atzerodt to the door. Atzerodt said Booth threatened him, and the physical action is ours.
	•	Who stood where in the room, and Herold's watch.
	•	Whether all four were in the room together at 8 pm is itself an "accounts say" (see §5).

John Wilkes Booth
JOHN WILKES BOOTH · America's matinee idol · 26
Widely called the handsomest man in America. Jet-black curly hair, a black mustache, pale skin, dark eyes; about 5'8", an athlete famous for leaping onto the stage. Dressed like a star. (His brother Edwin was the more famous tragedian; John was the matinee idol.) Casting: magnetic, never a heartthrob. He is a racist murderer who happened to be handsome.
Abraham Lincoln
ABRAHAM LINCOLN · President · 56
His own 1859 sketch: "six feet, four inches, nearly; lean in flesh… dark complexion, with coarse black hair, and grey eyes." By April 1865 he is gaunt, deeply lined and hollow-cheeked, with a chin beard and no mustache. VOICE (locked casting note): witnesses describe it as high-pitched, thin and reedy, a tenor that carried, with a Kentucky accent. Never the deep movie baritone. It's also a share fact.
Lewis Powell
LEWIS POWELL · Confederate veteran · 20
About 6'2", powerfully built and strikingly handsome, with a heavy jaw, thick dark hair and a flat, unblinking stare. His 1865 Gardner photos went viral in our time. Quiet, almost mute on screen.
David Herold
DAVID HEROLD · druggist's clerk · 22
Boyish, slight, eager. Booth's errand boy. He knew the back roads of Maryland.
George Atzerodt
GEORGE ATZERODT · carriage painter · 29
Small, stooped and scruffy, with a German accent (born in Prussia). The weak link.
William Seward
WILLIAM SEWARD · Secretary of State · 63
Hawk-nosed, frail, in bed in a metal-and-leather brace after the carriage crash.
Mary Surratt
MARY SURRATT · boarding-house owner · 42
A widow in black who ran the H Street boarding house where the plotters met. Contemporary descriptions give a stout, upright, dark-haired woman with grey eyes; she was devout. Only a few photographs are attributed to her, and their attribution is debated, so her face is the one we design rather than match. The series page says so.

Medium-high. Sources describe the device differently, and some say he also rolled off the bed.

The Vice President's assassin got drunk instead. Atzerodt took a room at Johnson's hotel, drank at the bar and never knocked.

Booth left a calling card at the Vice President's hotel that afternoon: "Don't wish to disturb you. Are you at home? J. Wilkes Booth." It ended up in the box of Johnson's secretary, William A. Browning. Whether it was meant for Johnson or for Browning, and why, historians still argue.

High (the card); open (the recipient and the motive)

Booth's brother had saved Lincoln's son. Edwin Booth pulled Robert Todd Lincoln off the tracks at the Jersey City station, months before the murder.
Robert Lincoln's 1909 letter to Century Magazine
High that it happened. The date is uncertain, 1863–65 (often given as Aug 1864).

It started as a kidnapping. On March 17, 1865, the gang waited on a road to grab Lincoln's carriage. He changed his plans.
Trial testimony; Kauffman
High
7
The trigger was a speech. On April 11, Lincoln publicly backed voting rights for some Black men. Booth, in the crowd, reportedly said it would be "the last speech he will ever make."

High (the speech); medium (Booth's words)

Booth waited for the biggest laugh in the play ("you sockdologizing old man-trap") so the shot would be covered by the laughter.
Our American Cousin script; Kauffman, Alford
High (the line); medium-high (the intent)
9
Powell walked into a trap. Three nights later he knocked on Mary Surratt's door with a pickaxe, claiming he'd come to dig a gutter, while detectives were inside arresting her. She said: "Before God, sir, I do not know this man."
Trial testimony
High
10
The one who got away. John Surratt fled to Canada and then Rome, enlisted in the Pope's army, jumped off a cliff to escape arrest, and was caught in Egypt. His 1867 jury deadlocked, and he lived free until 1916. His mother, Mary, was hanged in 1865, the first woman executed by the federal government.
Surratt trial 1867; Larson

	•	Henry Rathbone, the major in the box whom Booth stabbed, later killed his wife Clara Harris (who had been with him in the box) in 1883 and died in an asylum. High.
	•	Booth's last words in the burning barn were "Useless, useless." High.
	•	The man who shot Booth, Boston Corbett, fired against orders. Years earlier he had castrated himself. High.
	•	Lincoln had seen Booth act at Ford's in The Marble Heart, November 1863. High.
	•	"Now he belongs to the ages." Stanton may have said "angels" instead. Flag: disputed.
